# Probe types — four questions, and what each one can prove

A probe is a question whose answer is evidence. The four below are ordered by what they cost the user:
the open question costs words, the boundary costs a comparison, the counterexample costs a judgement,
and the transfer costs a thought. Use the cheapest one that can settle the status, and stop there.

| # | Type | Proves | Never proves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `open` | that a statement exists, in his words | that the statement is right |
| 2 | `boundary` | that the line is drawn and not just the term known | that he can use it on a new case |
| 3 | `counterexample` | that the boundary is usable on a case the text does not contain | that it transfers to his own domain |
| 4 | `transfer` | that the concept is his, not the text's | — it is the ceiling |

A concept is `clear` only after a 3 or a 4. Types 1 and 2 can establish `partial` at best, because both
can be answered by rearranging the text's own sentences.

---

## 1. `open` — the statement in his own words

**Construction.** Name the concept, ban the text's phrasing, ask for one sentence. The ban is the
question: *without the text's sentence* is what stops a recitation from passing as an answer.

- **Good:** "Say what opportunity cost means in one sentence, in your own words — not the paper's
  sentence."
- **Fails as:** "Tell me your understanding of opportunity cost." — no ban, so the text answers.

What to listen for: does the sentence contain a **relation** (costs what, against what, when), or only
the term and an adjective? "Opportunity cost is the thing you give up" has the term and nothing else.
That is `unclear`, not `partial` — a term with no relation is not a partial definition, it is a missing
one.

---

## 2. `boundary` — the line, not the label

**Construction.** Two items, one inside and one outside, both plausible to someone who has only met the
term. Ask which is which, and why. The *why* carries the evidence; the choice alone does not.

- **Good:** "You skip the trip and save 10,000, but you work three extra days. Are those three days part
  of the opportunity cost? Why?"
- **Fails as:** "How does opportunity cost differ from accounting cost?" — asks for the text's
  contrast, not for the line.

The pair must be drawn from the material's own boundary, not invented. If the text says "not everything
that is given up", the outside item is something that was given up and is still not part of the cost. A
pair built from the agent's general knowledge tests the agent's knowledge.

---

## 3. `counterexample` — a case the text does not contain

**Construction.** Take the boundary just established and instantiate it in a situation with a different
subject matter, a different scale, or a different time frame. It must be answerable, and it must not be
solvable by search.

- **Good:** "Your company spent two years building a feature nobody bought. Do those two years count as
  a sunk cost? Why can't they decide what you do next?"
- **Fails as:** "Should sunk costs affect decisions?" — a yes/no on the text's own sentence.

The good version has one right answer *and* a wrong one that a reader who memorised the sentence would
give. That asymmetry is the whole test: a probe where both answers are defensible measures rhetoric.

---

## 4. `transfer` — his own domain

**Construction.** Ask him for the case, not for the answer to a case. "In your work — or in your
course — is there a situation that is X? Name one." The case he produces is the evidence; a case the
agent supplies is not.

- **Good:** "In your internship, is there a decision that continued because of money already spent? Name
  one, and say what you would have decided if that money were ignored."
- **Fails as:** an example the agent offers for him to confirm. Confirming somebody else's case is
  recognition, and recognition is what a multiple-choice question already measures.

A transfer that lands is the only place the record can say `clear` on evidence rather than on style.

---

## What never counts as a probe

- **Recall questions.** "What is the definition?" "What are the three conditions?" — asks the text back,
  measures the text.
- **Two unknowns in one sentence.** "How does X differ from Y, and how would you use it?" — the second
  half is never answered.
- **Leading questions.** "Is it because of the next-best alternative?" — measures compliance.
- **Questions containing the answer's vocabulary.** If the question needs the text's term to be asked,
  the answer can only be the term. Ask with the situation's words instead.
- **A calculation dressed as a question.** Compute it and ask what he does with the number; arithmetic
  is not understanding.

---

## The `unclear` ladder

When the answer does not commit, the next question goes **down**, not sideways. Three rungs, in order:

1. **An analogy from his own life.** "If ..., is that the same as X?" — same structure, plainer costume.
2. **The two-reading question.** Two readings of one case, one true and one false, both taken from the
   material's boundary: "Is this one X, or is that one X? Why?" This is the only place the skill offers
   options, and the *why* is what is graded.
3. **The smallest case.** One subject, one decision, one sentence of setup.

Do not re-ask the same sentence with a louder voice. A re-asked question produces a re-worded answer,
and both go into the record looking like progress.

---

## Worked rewrites

| Too vague | Too narrow (supplies the answer) | The probe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Do you understand what an externality is?" | "Is an externality a cost borne by a third party?" | "Your factory sits next to a fish farm. You discharge waste, the fish die. Whose cost in that story never appears on your invoice?" |
| "Talk about economies of scale." | "Is economies of scale when bigger output means lower unit cost?" | "Two plants, one makes 10,000 units and one makes 100,000. The larger one is 30% cheaper per unit — is that scale, or is it because it buys its inputs cheaper? How would you tell them apart?" |
| "Have you heard of path dependence?" | "Is path dependence when earlier choices constrain the present?" | "The QWERTY keyboard is not the fastest layout. Why is it still here? Same question for your internship: is there a step in the process that survives for that reason?" |

The right column shares one property: it can be answered without the text's vocabulary, and it cannot be
answered without the text's distinction.
